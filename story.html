Why a game made of sound

My name is Russell Hinderman, and I am totally blind. I play games by ear, and I wanted a game that was built for that from the very beginning, not one where sound was added on top of a picture. In Endless Labyrinth there is nothing to see. Everything you need is in the sound: where something is, how far away it is, and whether it is in front of you or behind you.

That also decided how the game was made. Every change, in every mode, was tested the same way a player would meet it: by me, with headphones on, listening.

This page was written with Claude's help, from my own words, my decisions and the record of how the game was made. The ideas and the choices in it are mine; the writing was done the way the game itself is made, with me directing and checking it.

What AI changed for me

I first learned to code by taking a Python course. It was all about how to type syntax and what to do when there were syntax errors. I realized from the very start that typing syntax was extremely exhausting for me. I hated going to the computer just to start typing things out.

When I began using AI to build things, I was pretty skeptical. Even when I used Google's Gemini to make games, a lot of them had errors and bugs, and I kept having to correct the model. Then, at the Medina County Career Center, where I still study, my instructor showed me Claude. Since I started using Claude, I have never looked back. It has helped me create products I am truly proud of, like a digital audio workstation and this game, Endless Labyrinth.

Where it started

Before Endless Labyrinth there was Sonar Escape, a concept I made a long time ago with Gemini, Google's AI. It no longer exists, and it was simple: you found the exit by listening to a tone that rose in pitch the closer you got, and you collected credits on the way. That was the first version of the idea that grew into the game you can play today.

I tried other prototypes with Gemini around the same time, among them a murder mystery, a tower you climbed floor by floor, an ocean dive where you went underwater to catch fish, and Escape the Cave, where an elevator took you up and down a cave and dropped you into random passages, and you collected items to open the elevator door and get out. None of them exist any more, but looking back, I can hear them in Endless Labyrinth today: in Tower mode, in Island mode's dives and elevators, and in the fishing events.

I started Endless Labyrinth in the summer of 2026, and it also started simply, as one idea that gave the game its name: you are in a labyrinth you cannot see, and you have to find the exit by ear before the clock runs out. You step through it one square at a time. A thud tells you a step worked, and a knock tells you that you walked into a wall. The exit beeps, panning left and right to give you its direction, and the beep rises in pitch and speeds up as you get closer. Every labyrinth you finish adds a little time and leads to the next, and it never ends. That is still Standard mode today.

The very first version of Endless Labyrinth, that simple labyrinth with its proximity beeping, was made in Google Gemini. Then the work carried on in Claude, an AI made by Anthropic, and everything the game has become was built there. I wrote it for Windows, in Python, with the pygame library playing the sound. I do not write the code by hand: I design the game and direct the AI to write it, then play every build and decide what to keep, what to change and what to throw out. How that works is described below, in How I make the game.

Growing into six modes

Once finding an exit by ear worked, I wanted to see how far the same idea could go. Each new mode asks your ears to do something different.

  • Battle took away the walls and the exit. Enemies come at you in waves in an open arena, and you aim by turning until an enemy sounds centred between your ears. Its clock went through several versions before it felt right: no limit at first, then three minutes, then thirty seconds, and finally sixty seconds that keep running through the breaks, with time credits only between waves.
  • Tower became a climb. Each floor has a rival that throws at you and competes with you for the items, and the stairs only appear once the rival is down.
  • Driving put you on a three-lane road, dodging or shooting oncoming cars. I wanted it to get harder forever, with no top level, and crashes costing time to balance it. In October I found I could survive more than seven minutes, so we made credits and ammo rarer and kept the difficulty endless.
  • Island came from an idea I had on 28 October: cross a chain of islands, clear each one, and dive to the seabed for the key and the ammo before your air runs out. It grew elevators that carry you down and up, guardians, trapped divers and treasure, and the sounds of water and the seabed.
  • Chaos started as a mode of its own and was rebuilt into what it is now: every other mode mixed together, one random level after another, against a single five-minute clock, with no warning of what comes next.

As the modes grew, I set rules so they would always feel like one game. Space is always the attack and Enter is always the report, in every mode that attacks. In chaos, each kind of level keeps its own ammo. And anything that changes in a mode has to change in chaos too.

Because I test on a BrailleNote, a notetaker with a Braille display, the Windows version also learned to show everything it says on a Braille display, and to work with the BrailleNote's own keys.

Building a world out of sound

At first most of the game's sounds were generated by the program itself: beeps, tones and chimes. At the end of October we did a big overhaul of how the game sounds.

  • Real recordings. Most one-off sounds, such as pickups, shots, explosions, coins and the gong at the end of a wave, became real recordings from Pixabay. The beacons and enemies you track by ear kept their generated sounds, because their exact pitch and rhythm are how you find them. The game now uses 348 recordings: 313 in both versions and 35 more in the website alone. The Credits page lists every one by mode, along with nine more that were downloaded but never used, such as car engines tried for driving mode and a spare coin.
  • Room echo. I wanted every room to sound like a place, but only slightly, so nothing ever becomes harder to hear. We looked at real 3D audio libraries and chose a slight echo built into the game instead. Each room's echo matches the air of that level, gets a little stronger in a corridor or by a wall, and comes from where the sound is. Islands, the seabed and the open road stay dry, except in a road's tunnels and under its overpasses.
  • Behind you. At first, something in front of you and something behind you sounded the same. Now north is always ahead, and anything behind you sounds duller, as if heard over your shoulder.
  • The voice in the room. On Windows, I suggested that the game's voice could sit in the room too. It does: each sentence is spoken into a sound file and played back with that room's echo.
  • One rule. The recordings and generated sounds themselves are never changed. Any effect, like an echo or the dull sound of something behind you, is a copy played alongside the original.

Events

On Halloween 2026 I wanted the game to have something new to come back to. My first idea was a Pumpkin Hunt: find as many pumpkins as you can in a room as big as a cathedral, in two minutes. That became the event system.

Events are limited-time challenges that I publish. Each one has its own mission, its own dates and its own best score, and the Event High Scores never reset. I build them in my own tool, the Event Maker, which uses real accessible controls, and every event is signed with a key only I have, so nobody else can change them. There are now thirty kinds of mission, from hunting and rescuing to rhythm and fishing, and more than a hundred templates to build events from. Call of Fate, a hunt for the real bell among false ones, and The Angry Papa, my own cookie-collecting event, came out of this.

Release

On 1 November 2026 I put Endless Labyrinth on GitHub, under the name DevHinderman2026, and released version 1.0.0 for Windows. I chose to keep the game's code private and all rights reserved: players download and play. The sounds travel only inside the game, and events reach players straight from the repository, so a new event never needs a new download.

The next day I released 1.0.1. Before it, I decided to fix every known problem in one release. The game went through eighteen review rounds, many of them by independent reviewers that had never seen the code, and hundreds of fixes, from how hits are counted to how events save. I consider the Windows game complete at 1.0.1.

The Windows version is still the full game on a computer, with Braille display support and the voice in the room. You can download it from the Releases page, and read what each Windows version changed in its changelog.

Phones, tablets and computers

On 2 November I wanted to play Endless Labyrinth on my phone. I designed the touch controls myself: swipes are the arrow keys, a double tap is Enter, a two-finger tap is the attack, and in driving you slide a finger up and hold it to accelerate. The game speaks with your phone's own voice while the screen reader is off, and since web version 6.0.0, during a run, with a voice of its own.

The first web version had only Standard and Battle. I tested them on my phone, they worked well, and the same day I decided to bring over everything: all six modes and the events. Every mode was converted from the Windows code line by line and checked against it in two rounds of review, with the same rules, timings and sounds.

Playing on a real iPhone found what reviews could not. In Chaos, the sound could disappear a few levels into a run, so the way the game builds its room echoes was rebuilt to suit iPhones; that was version 1.0.2. With 1.0.3 the web version also plays on Chromebooks, Macs and Linux computers, with the keyboard. Windows players keep the full Windows version, and since web 5.0.0 a Windows computer can also play the whole game on the website. Every web version is listed in the Update History, and About and how to play explains every mode, gesture and key.

Ultimate Mode

By November the web version had everything the Windows game had, and I had an idea that could only be done on the web: a mode harder than Chaos. Chaos tests whether you can switch between modes. I wanted a mode where every environment is mixed. Not completing a task and then going to another mode, but one huge environment that you have to navigate and try to make it through: ammo scattered around in certain places and enemies to shoot, labyrinths to find your way out of, islands to walk on with the sounds fading in as you get close, enemies that throw things at you. All of it at once, and the test is survival, not speed. No driving, because a car has nothing to do in a place you walk through. I called it Ultimate Mode, and I kept the name after considering others, because it tells you exactly what it is.

The web made it possible because a browser has true binaural 3D sound built in. With headphones it can place a sound in front of you, behind you, or below you, not only left and right. At first the rest of the game stayed in stereo, matching Windows, but a world this big needed the 3D, and the elevator down to an island's seabed is where you hear it best: the water rises from beneath you. Every area of the world has its own echo, sized to the area, and crossing a border blends from one to the next.

The world regenerates. At a random moment, one to four minutes in and with nothing but a five-second countdown to warn you, the whole thing is torn down and built again at random, and you have to find your bearings again by ear, with the ammo and health you had. I wanted that because finding out where you are is the heart of this game, and a world you could learn would stop testing it. The safe zones sound like the places from the Windows game's events, which I think are an important part of making the worlds sound real; the compass, which you turn on by holding a finger still, points the walkable route to the nearest one. I designed all of this in a conversation with Claude on 8 November 2026, question by question, and we wrote it into a plan before any code was touched; then Claude's Fable model built it from that plan as web version 2.0.0, testing it in a browser as it went, and committed it on my laptop for me to publish. It is the first mode made only for the web, and for a while it was the one mode a Windows computer could play on the website. Since web version 5.0.0 a Windows computer plays the whole game there.

Turning, and a voice of its own

Ultimate Mode taught me something: once you can turn where you stand and hear the world sweep round you, going back to north always ahead feels like walking in a box. So on 12 November, with web version 5.0.0, the whole web game moved into binaural 3D, and every walking mode began to turn with you: you turn until a sound is centred between your ears and bright, then walk to it. Driving and the events kept their old controls, because a road and a room built square to north make sense that way. Turning made some things harder, so the clocks, the enemies and the rival in the towers were all rebalanced around it, and I kept retuning them by ear until each mode felt fair again.

The same day I released Windows version 3.0.0, with a far bigger event system: creatures that listen for you, glass cases to smash, floors you can hear under your feet, and many new missions. Its signature event, Trapped in Darkness, was inspired by Papa Sangre II: you search for lost memories, each one a few notes of music, while creatures wait in the dark and charge when they hear you.

On 19 November, web version 6.0.0 brought a new mode, Find the Voice. A woman's voice calls to you from somewhere in a dark room, and you find her by ear, again and again, while a blind creature listens for your steps on broken glass. It also gave the game a voice of its own during a run, Bella, made with Kokoro, an open speech model, and gave every character in Ultimate Mode's safe zones a voice of their own. At first those voices were made live on your phone, and sometimes the phone fell back to its own voice. That was not good enough for the characters, so in version 6.1.0, the same day, every line the game says during a run became a recording, made once and played the same on every device.

The Abyss

On 20 November I wanted something closer to the games that inspired me: a place that frightens you only through what you hear. The Abyss is an endless abyss with no walls and no edge, and the whole floor is broken glass, so every step you take can be heard. Blind creatures hunt you by sound. Lightning is your cover: every few seconds it strikes somewhere far off, with a real thunderclap, the creatures run toward it, and for five seconds your steps go unheard. Your goal is to smash as many glass cases as you can before the clock runs out. The first thunder I was given was a low rumble I could barely hear, so I chose real thunderclaps instead, a different one every strike. It became web version 6.2.0, and The Abyss came to Chaos mode too.

Hunting the static

Not every chapter is a new feature. From 19 November, playing on my iPhone, I heard a slight static creeping onto the edges of the sounds the longer I played, and pausing the game made it vanish. It is the hardest problem the game has had, because it cannot be heard anywhere but on a real phone, and only I could hear it.

Each search found a real cause. The seabed's water was playing more than twice as loud as it was made to, so the game was always straining at the top. Sounds that had moved once kept the phone working on them for good. Every sound ever placed stayed connected inside the phone's audio engine, and a five-minute Chaos run left about thirteen hundred of them. Sounds you could no longer hear were still being worked on as if you could. Each fix made it better, and some fixes caused new problems, like a popping in the labyrinths that had to be undone. Version 6.3.7, on 22 November, made the static much less likely, but did not solve it, and pausing and resuming the game still cleared it.

That clue turned out to be the key. Pausing is the one moment in a run when the phone's own voice speaks, saying Game paused, because while the game is paused its recorded voice is silent. When the phone's voice starts, the phone restarts its sound output. The game had been making its sound at 44,100 samples a second, the rate of its recordings, while an iPhone plays at 48,000, so the phone was converting every sound on its way out for as long as the game was open, and that converter in the iPhone's browser is known to crackle more and more as it runs. Since version 6.1.0 nearly every line in a run had been a recording, so nothing else in a run ever restarted it, and the static first crept in the day after 6.1.0. In version 7.0.0, on 23 November, the game makes its sound at the device's own rate, and each recording is converted only once, when it is loaded.

For this hunt, Claude and I changed how we work. Claude's Opus model reviews the whole game and writes every fix it believes is right into a plan. Its Fable model then checks each fix against the code before building it: if a fix is right, it builds it; if a fact is wrong, it corrects the plan first and tells me why. Automated players play every mode and every event, pausing and resuming hundreds of times, and the plan records what was measured. When the numbers change how the game plays, the decision comes back to me. For the islands, the planned ammo rule looked fair on paper, but measured over twenty thousand pretend runs it left careful players short, so I chose a bigger spare.

Water Park

The Abyss frightens you; I wanted a mode where you simply have fun, and still a challenge. Water Park, version 7.0.0, is a big water park. Every attraction holds a token, the water slide, the diving pool, the wave pool and the tipping bucket, each found by its own sound, and every token must be won before closing time. Then a gate opens to the next park, which is bigger. Nothing in the park can hurt you: a mistake brings the lifeguard's whistle and costs a few seconds. The whole park rings like a huge indoor hall, and each attraction calls out to you: the slide's stairs, the bouncing diving board, the breaking waves and the bucket's bell. Its new sounds, the busy pool, the bucket, the waves, the diving board and the whistle, came from Pixabay as always, and it came to Chaos Mode too, one park to a level. In version 7.1.0 the park got a fifth attraction, the duck stand: you take an air rifle at the counter of a carnival stand, its organ playing, and shoot down the ducks that fly in at you from all around.

How I make the game

Endless Labyrinth is AI assisted, and I am open about how. I am the designer and the tester. Claude writes the code. I have worked this way since the game's first days, when its first version was made in Gemini, and over time it has become a method.

The tools

  • Claude, by Anthropic. I use its Opus model for most of the building, including this web version, and its Fable model for the hardest bug hunting and for the biggest builds, such as the 2.0.0 difficulty overhaul and Ultimate Mode.
  • The Claude desktop app, linked to my laptop, so Claude works on the game's files on my own computer.
  • GitHub, where the game is published. I publish with GitHub Desktop, and Claude can also publish for me once I have decided a version is ready; nothing goes public before I say so.
  • Headphones, my BrailleNote and my iPhone, for testing: the Windows version on my laptop and on the BrailleNote with its Braille display, and the web version on my phone.

From idea to build

  • I describe what I want in plain words, often as a scene: a cathedral full of pumpkins, an island you can only cross by diving, a road that gets faster forever.
  • Claude asks before it builds. Anything that could go more than one way comes back to me as a question, one at a time, with choices I can pick or answer in my own words. For a big piece of work, such as the 1.0.1 bug fixes, we agree a written plan before any code is touched.
  • Every decision about how the game plays is mine: the clocks, the controls, the sounds, the rules, and what to remove. Claude gives me its honest opinion, and sometimes talks me out of something, but I decide.
  • Files are handled carefully. Claude backs up what it changes, checks every file it copies to my laptop against a fingerprint so I know it arrived exactly as written, and refreshes a backup copy in OneDrive at the end of each session.

How it is tested

  • By ear, by me. I play every build with headphones and report what I hear, down to a bell I could ring from the side when I should have had to walk into it, or footsteps that dipped while I walked on my phone. Many fixes started that way.
  • Automated tests. Claude writes test programs that play the game by themselves: bots that walk labyrinths, fight battles and cross islands, and thousands of random sessions run to catch crashes. Before Windows 1.0.1, 374 automated checks passed. The web version has its own tests, which play every mode in a browser and check each kind of phone, tablet and computer.
  • Independent reviews. Before a release, separate Claude reviewers that have not seen the work read the code from scratch and report what they find. Windows 1.0.1 went through eighteen review rounds, and the web version was checked against the Windows code in two rounds.
  • The real thing. Some problems only show up on a real device, like the Chaos sound on iPhone. That is why I play every release myself before calling it done.

How Claude learned to write code

People often ask how an AI can write a whole game. Anthropic has not published every detail of how it trains Claude, but it has said what goes into it, and the general method is well described in research.

  • Learning from a huge amount of text, including code. Anthropic's published summary for Claude Opus 5 says it was trained on more than ten trillion tokens of text, including software code. Its sources include the public web, open-source software and GitHub, along with licensed data, synthetic data, and chats and coding sessions from users who chose to share them. [1, 2]
  • Practice with feedback. After that first stage, a model is refined with reinforcement learning: it tries, and is rewarded for better answers. Anthropic's own method, Constitutional AI, combines people's judgements of how helpful an answer is with feedback guided by a written set of principles. [3] For code in particular, researchers train models by running the programs they write against tests and rewarding the ones that pass. [4, 5] Anthropic has not said exactly how it does this for Claude, so this describes the general approach, not Claude's exact recipe.
  • It still makes mistakes. That is why every build is played by ear, tested and reviewed before it reaches you.

Building it this way means I could make the game I wanted to play, the way I wanted it to sound. I hope you enjoy playing it as much as I have enjoyed making it.
